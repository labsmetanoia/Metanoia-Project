Chapter I · The Turn

At some point in every successful career, performance is no longer the question. Direction is.

There is a specific kind of career crisis that doesn’t look like a crisis from the outside. The title is respectable. The income is real. The colleagues are good. But somewhere in the middle of a Wednesday, a question surfaces that is difficult to dismiss: is this still the right direction?

The professionals who navigate this moment well are not the ones who ignore the question or make impulsive decisions. They are the ones who bring the same rigor to evaluating their career that they would bring to any complex professional problem — with data, with frameworks, and with the honesty to act on what they find.

“Twelve years of being good at something you are no longer sure you chose is not a verdict. It is a starting position — once you can see it clearly.”

What professionals who successfully reinvent themselves do

01They separate identity from roleThey understand that what they do is not who they are — and this distinction is what makes bold moves possible.
02They audit their career capital explicitlyThey know what their experience is worth in the market, not just within their current organization.
03They engage their network as intelligenceThey use their network to test hypotheses about new directions, not just to find job openings.
04They manage transition risk systematicallyThey make changes in sequence, not all at once — building the bridge before abandoning the current path.
05They invest in new contexts deliberatelyThey take board roles, advisory positions, or executive education programs that expand their aperture.
06They define the next chapter before they need itThe best career transitions happen from a position of strength, not urgency.

None of this requires abandoning what you’ve built. It requires seeing it differently: as capital to be redeployed, not a track to be endured.

There are three proven routes at the senior level: an internal transfer through a multinational you already work for, a direct market entry built on a specialised and demonstrable expertise, or an education-bridge through an international program. Which route fits depends on your industry, your visa realities, and how portable your track record is. The Repositioning product helps you assess all three honestly — including the compensation, family, and timeline trade-offs that matter far more at this stage than they did at 25.
The de-risking principle is sequence: never make all the changes at once. The framework in Strategic Repositioning stages the move — validate the venture idea while employed, secure 12–18 months of runway, build the first customers or partnerships before resigning, and design a fallback that preserves your corporate re-entry value. Entrepreneurs who transition this way keep their leverage; those who leap first and plan second are betting their family’s runway on their most optimiztic scenario.

An EMBA reliably delivers three things: a credential for roles that gatekeep on it, a senior peer network, and structured strategic breadth. It does not reliably deliver a promotion by itself. The LPDP & Executive Education product runs the ROI calculation for your specific situation: your target role, whether it actually gatekeeps on the credential, the true cost including two years of evenings, and the alternatives — targeted executive education, board readiness programs, or deliberate experience — that often deliver the same outcome faster.
Senior networks are usually wide but pointed backwards — dense in the sector you’re leaving, thin in the one you’re entering. The repositioning approach treats your network as intelligence first and introductions second: use trusted contacts to test your transition hypothesis, understand how the target sector actually evaluates outsiders, and identify the two or three people whose endorsement carries weight there. Then build toward those people deliberately, months before you need anything from them. Asking for a job is the last step of network leverage, not the first.
C-suite negotiation is less about salary bands and more about scope, mandate, and equity of position: what you will own, what resources come with it, how success will be measured, and what happens if the strategy changes above you. The Executive Presence Program prepares the negotiation itself — the language, the sequencing, and the leverage points — while the Leadership Assessment gives you the evidence base: a documented picture of the value you bring that turns the conversation from asking into offering.
